Most people who type for a living have never actually measured how fast they type. They have a vague sense of it — "pretty fast," "not great," "I hunt and peck a little" — but no real number. That's worth fixing, because typing speed is one of the few cognitive-motor skills you can measure precisely in under a minute, track over time, and genuinely improve with the right kind of practice. Here's what the numbers actually look like, and what's worth doing about them.

## What WPM Actually Measures

"Words per minute" sounds simple, but there are two different numbers hiding under that one label, and mixing them up is the most common source of confusion.

### Gross WPM

This counts every group of five keystrokes (including spaces) as one "word," regardless of whether you typed it correctly. It's a measure of raw motor speed — how fast your fingers can move — with no penalty for mistakes.

### Net WPM (Adjusted WPM)

This is gross WPM minus a penalty for errors, usually calculated by subtracting one word for every mistake still present in the final text. Net WPM is the number that actually matters for real-world typing, because a blazing-fast typist who has to stop and fix ten typos per paragraph isn't actually faster than a more careful typist in practice — they're just moving their hands faster.

Most typing tests, including ours, report net WPM alongside your raw speed and accuracy percentage, because that combination tells you far more than either number alone.

## Average Typing Speed by Skill Level

Typing speed research and large-scale test data (from sites that have logged millions of typing sessions) converge on a similar rough distribution:

- **Under 20 WPM** — Beginner. Still relies heavily on looking at the keyboard, or new to touch typing entirely.
- **20–40 WPM** — Below average to average. This covers most casual computer users who type regularly but never specifically trained on it.
- **40–60 WPM** — Above average. Typical for people who type extensively for work — writers, students, office staff — without formal typing training.
- **60–80 WPM** — Fast. Usually reflects either touch-typing training or years of high-volume typing (support agents, transcriptionists, programmers).
- **80–100 WPM** — Very fast. Found among professional typists, competitive typists, and people who've deliberately trained for speed.
- **100+ WPM** — Elite. The realistic ceiling for sustained, accurate typing; competitive typing records go well beyond this, but those are short bursts under ideal conditions, not representative of everyday typing.

If you're wondering where you land: 40 WPM is a reasonable middle-of-the-road benchmark for an adult who uses a keyboard regularly but has never trained specifically for speed. If your result comes back below that, it's not unusual — it just means there's real room to improve with a bit of deliberate practice, not that something is wrong.

## Does Typing Speed Change With Age?

Broadly, yes, but the pattern isn't a simple straight line, and cause and effect run in more than one direction.

**Children and teens** typically type well below adult averages, mostly because of less total keyboard exposure and still-developing fine motor control, not any cognitive limitation. Kids who grow up with heavy keyboard or touchscreen use from an early age (a much larger share of each new generation) tend to reach higher speeds earlier than previous generations did.

**Adults in their 20s through 40s** generally post the highest average speeds, reflecting both peak fine motor coordination and (for many people) the most cumulative years of regular keyboard use through school and work.

**Older adults** often show a gradual decline in raw typing speed, which tracks a broader, well-documented pattern of slightly slower fine motor control and reaction time with age. This is a small, gradual effect for most healthy older adults, not a dramatic one — and it's frequently offset by decades of accumulated typing experience and more efficient, practiced movement patterns. Many experienced older typists still comfortably out-type younger, less-practiced ones.

The bigger factor by far is practice volume and technique, not age on its own. Someone who types eight hours a day will consistently out-pace someone who types for ten minutes, at any age.

## Average Typing Speed by Profession

Typing speed expectations vary a lot by job, and some professions effectively require touch typing to function well:

- **General office / admin work** — 40–50 WPM
- **Customer support / live chat** — 50–65 WPM
- **Data entry specialist** — 60–80 WPM, often with high accuracy requirements
- **Court reporter / stenographer** — 180–225+ WPM, using specialized shorthand keyboards, not standard ones
- **Transcriptionist** — 65–85 WPM
- **Professional writer / journalist** — 50–70 WPM
- **Software developer** — highly variable; code isn't prose, so raw WPM matters less than typing accuracy and command of keyboard shortcuts

Court reporters are a useful reality check on what "fast" really means: their extreme speeds come from a completely different input method (steno keyboards that type syllables and phonetic chords, not individual letters), not from superhuman finger speed on a standard QWERTY layout.

## Accuracy Matters More Than People Think

It's tempting to chase a bigger WPM number, but accuracy has an outsized effect on real typing throughput. Consider two typists working on the same document:

- Typist A: 70 WPM raw speed, 85% accuracy — spends significant time going back to find and fix errors.
- Typist B: 55 WPM raw speed, 98% accuracy — rarely stops, moves through the document almost linearly.

In real, sustained typing (not a 15-second speed test), Typist B often finishes comparable work faster and with far less fatigue, because error correction is expensive: it breaks flow, requires re-reading, and compounds as documents get longer. This is exactly why our test reports accuracy and net WPM together rather than just raw speed — a high raw number with mediocre accuracy isn't actually the win it looks like.

## What Actually Improves Typing Speed

Some commonly repeated typing advice holds up under scrutiny; some doesn't. Here's what the evidence and expert consensus generally support:

1. **Learn proper touch-typing technique first.** Home-row finger placement and typing without looking at the keys is consistently the single biggest lever for people who currently "hunt and peck." This alone often produces the largest speed jump of any single change.
2. **Practice with real, varied text — not just drills.** Repeatedly typing the same short phrases builds narrow muscle memory that doesn't transfer well. Practicing with varied, natural sentences (the way our test passages are built) more closely matches real typing demands.
3. **Prioritize accuracy while you're building the habit, then let speed follow.** Trying to type fast before your fingers know where the keys are just cements bad habits and errors that are hard to unlearn later. Accuracy-first practice tends to produce faster, more durable speed gains than speed-first practice.
4. **Type regularly, not in occasional long bursts.** Short, frequent practice sessions (10–15 minutes most days) generally build motor memory more effectively than one long session per week, which is consistent with how motor skill learning works more broadly.
5. **Check your posture and keyboard position.** Awkward wrist angles and poor posture don't just risk strain injuries — they also physically limit how efficiently your fingers can move. This is a genuinely underrated factor.

What doesn't hold up as well: speed-typing "hacks" that promise huge gains in a few days with no real practice, or claims that any single keyboard layout (Dvorak, Colemak, etc.) will dramatically outperform a well-practiced QWERTY typist. Layout differences are real but modest for most people; the technique and practice volume matter far more than the layout underneath it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is 40 WPM good?

It's right around the general adult average, so it's a solid, unremarkable baseline — not slow, not exceptional. Most jobs that involve regular typing are comfortable with this range.

### Is 60 WPM good?

Yes, meaningfully above average. This is a common target for roles that involve heavy typing, like customer support or content writing, and it's very achievable with a few weeks of consistent, technique-focused practice if you're starting lower.

### What is the world record for typing speed?

Officially recognized peak typing speed records sit above 200 WPM on standard keyboards, though these are short bursts on specific test passages under competition conditions — not a sustainable, everyday working speed for even highly skilled typists.

### Can I realistically improve my typing speed as an adult?

Yes. Fine motor skill learning doesn't stop after childhood. Adults who switch from hunt-and-peck to proper touch typing, or who add regular deliberate practice, commonly see meaningful gains within a few weeks — this is one of the more trainable skills people tend to underestimate.
